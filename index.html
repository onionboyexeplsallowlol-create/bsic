why bother

Two problems that don't usually meet each other.

Ash ponds

Coal power plants store leftover fly ash as wet slurry in large storage ponds. Left alone, those ponds can leak pollutants into nearby groundwater — a long-term liability that just sits there.

Polluted water

Meanwhile, communities everywhere need affordable ways to treat contaminated water — and conventional filter materials still have to be mined, shaped, and manufactured from scratch.

ash pond waste + water treatment need → AshPure ceramic filter

how it's made

From wet slurry to a working filter.

Real fly ash has to be dewatered, tested, and fired under controlled conditions — never handled raw. The steps below describe the actual manufacturing process; the school-safe version further down swaps in clay and charcoal.

01

Dewater the slurry

The pond slurry settles in a tank so heavier ash sinks and clear water rises. A belt filter press then squeezes the thick ash into a solid cake, separating most of the water out.

02

Dry, test, and grind

The ash cake is dried, checked for heavy metals and radioactivity, then ground into a fine, consistent powder.

03

Mix the paste

Ash is combined with clay or sand for structure, a pore-forming material like sawdust or starch, a binder, and water — forming a workable paste.

04

Shape and dry

The paste is pressed into a disk, tube, or plate and dried slowly, so it doesn't crack as the water leaves it.

05

Fire in a kiln

Heated to roughly 800–1150°C, the binder and pore-former burn away, leaving connected pores behind — and the ash and clay fuse into a hard ceramic.

06

Coat, install, and test

An optional layer of activated carbon or zeolite can be added for specific pollutants, then the filter is measured for pore size, strength, flow rate, and removal performance before installation.

what it could replace

Not everything — but a useful slice of it.

AshPure wouldn't stand in for every water filter. It would need to be engineered for the specific pollutant it's targeting. Where it fits, though, it could reduce the need for:

Conventional ceramic filter material
Clay used in filter production
Mined silica / alumina raw material
Some activated filter media
Disposable plastic filter cartridges

Other things coal ash can become

Zeolites

Porous minerals used for water purification, odour removal, and gas separation.

Glass-ceramic panels

Hard, glass-like tiles made from ash and other minerals.

Insulation foam

Lightweight, porous material that reduces heat transfer.

Pigments

Iron-rich ash processed into coloured coating materials.

Catalyst supports

Porous structures that help chemical reactions along.

Pervious pavement

Porous surfaces that let rainwater soak into the ground.

Do not drink the filtered water, and never use real ash from an ash pond.

be honest about it

What AshPure wouldn't fix.

AshPure mainly removes suspended particles and some larger contaminants — it wouldn't automatically make water safe to drink. Dissolved salts, microbes, and many chemicals pass straight through a plain ceramic membrane unless it carries an added treatment layer, and every version would need to be engineered and tested for the pollutant it's meant to catch.
