Solar still simulator

What carbonized date-palm waste actually does to a solar still

Salt doesn't pass through a biochar filter — it passes through a phase change. This simulator models the part date-palm biochar really plays: as a floating solar absorber that pulls more of the sun's energy into evaporation, so more seawater turns to vapor and leaves its salt behind.

The still, in motion

Brine sits under a sloped glass cover. A thin mat of carbonized date-palm fiber floats on the surface, soaking up sunlight at the interface instead of letting it scatter through the bulk water. Vapor rises, condenses on the glass, and drips into the collection channel — arriving with the salt left behind.

How this is modeled. Solar input follows a sine curve across the daylight window you set. Each absorber has an absorptance (how much sunlight it captures) and an evaporation efficiency (how much of that captured energy becomes vapor rather than warming the water or the air around it — the number carbonized biochar wins on, because its porous, dark structure traps heat right at the water's surface). Evaporated volume is tallied as fresh distillate; remaining salt concentrates in the shrinking brine until it hits NaCl's solubility limit (~360 g/L), at which point salt begins to crystallize out and evaporation from that basin is capped. This is a simplified energy-balance teaching model, not a substitute for lab measurement of a specific biochar sample.
